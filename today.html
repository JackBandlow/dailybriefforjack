Level Up

Sort each task by urgent vs important before you start

Before you open email or Slack, write today's tasks and mark each one urgent (needs action soon, with real consequences if delayed) or important (supports a real goal even if it can wait). Do items that are both first; schedule a concrete time for important-but-not-urgent work; hand off or batch urgent-but-not-important requests; drop the rest. Eisenhower drew the distinction in a 1954 speech, and Harvard Business Review cites research showing people often finish easy urgent tasks at the expense of more valuable ones.

Sources: The American Presidency Project · Harvard Business Review

Keep the same wake-up time every day, including days off

Pick one wake-up time and keep it on workdays and days off alike. CDC and NHLBI both advise going to bed and getting up at the same time every day, and NHLBI notes that sleeping in late on weekends can disrupt the body clock's sleep-wake rhythm. NHLBI also recommends using the hour before bed for quiet time and avoiding bright screens, because the light may signal the brain that it is time to be awake. Treat the wake time as the anchor even when bedtime slips.

Sources: CDC · NHLBI / NIH

Today's field: Science & technology

  • Diffraction. Diffraction is the bending and spreading of waves around obstacles or past openings. It happens with sound, light, and other waves, and it is easiest to notice when the obstacle or gap is about the size of the wavelength. That is why you can hear someone around a corner even when you cannot see them, and why light through a narrow slit fans into a pattern of bright and dark bands. Diffraction is evidence that light behaves as a wave, not only as a straight ray. [OpenStax University Physics Volume 3 · University of Alberta Physics]
  • Radioactive half-life. A radioactive half-life is the time it takes for half the radioactive atoms in a sample to decay into another form. After one half-life, half remain; after two, about one quarter remain, and so on. Each radioactive isotope has its own fixed half-life, which can be seconds or billions of years. Knowing the half-life helps doctors and nuclear workers estimate how long a radioactive material stays active. [CDC · U.S. EPA]
  • Signal-to-noise ratio. Signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) compares the strength of the useful signal to the background noise that rides with it. Engineers usually write it as a power ratio and often convert it to decibels so large and small values are easier to compare. A higher SNR means the message is easier to pick out; a low SNR means noise can hide or distort it. The same idea shows up in radios, microphones, medical imaging, and sensors: clearer readings need more signal, less noise, or both. [IEEE Technology Navigator · Rice University ECE]
