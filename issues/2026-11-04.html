Level Up

Know your walk-away option before any negotiation

Before a negotiation, write down your BATNA, your best alternative to a negotiated agreement: what you will actually do if this deal fails, such as another buyer, another job offer, or litigation. Fisher and Ury introduced the term in "Getting to Yes" (1981) as the standard for judging any proposal, so you accept offers better than your BATNA and decline those that are worse. Spend prep time improving your alternative (for example, getting a competing quote) and estimating the other side's BATNA, since both set the realistic bargaining range. Keep the written BATNA in front of you so pressure in the room does not move your floor.

Harvard Program on Negotiation · Wikipedia

Timebox your day instead of working from a to-do list

Timeboxing means assigning each task a fixed block on your calendar with a set end time, then stopping or re-planning when the block ends. A list says what to do; a timeboxed schedule also forces you to decide when and how long, which exposes overcommitment before the day starts. Cal Newport recommends giving every working hour a job and simply redrawing the plan when interruptions break it, rather than abandoning it. Try it tomorrow: spend ten minutes the night before blocking your top two tasks into the morning and batching small items into one 30-minute slot.

Today's field: Construction & engineering

  • Prestressed concrete. Concrete is very strong when squeezed but cracks easily when pulled or bent. Prestressing squeezes the concrete ahead of time by stretching high-strength steel tendons and locking them in place, either before the concrete is poured (pre-tensioning) or after it hardens (post-tensioning). When a load later tries to bend the beam, it first has to cancel that built-in squeeze before the concrete is in tension. This lets beams and slabs span farther with less material, which is why it is common in bridges and parking garages. FHWA Post-Tensioning Manual · Designing Buildings Wiki
  • Factor of safety. A factor of safety is how much stronger a structure or part is than it needs to be for the load it is expected to carry. It is usually expressed as a ratio, such as failure load divided by expected load. A rope that breaks at 3,000 pounds used to hold 1,000 pounds has a factor of safety of 3. Engineers use this margin to cover uncertainty in loads, materials, workmanship and wear, and building codes set minimums for many applications. MIT OpenCourseWare · Engineering ToolBox
  • Soil liquefaction. Loose, water-saturated sandy soil normally holds weight because its grains press against each other. Strong shaking, such as an earthquake, raises the water pressure between the grains so they lose contact and the soil briefly behaves like a thick liquid. Buildings can sink or tilt, and "sand boils" can erupt at the surface, as seen in Niigata, Japan in 1964. Engineers reduce the risk by compacting or draining the ground, or by using deep foundations that reach firmer layers. National Academies · UC Berkeley Seismology Lab
