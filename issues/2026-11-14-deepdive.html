Gold falls to a one-year low as the dollar hits a 19-month high

Gold prices fell on Thursday and closed at a one-year low, as a stronger US dollar and growing expectations of a Federal Reserve interest-rate hike weighed on demand for the metal. The decline followed stronger-than-expected US inflation data released on Tuesday, which showed consumer prices accelerating in October. Hawkish remarks from Federal Reserve officials further strengthened expectations of monetary tightening.

Spot gold fell 0.2% to $3,981.49 per ounce by 0310 GMT, after touching its lowest level since November 4 earlier in the session. US gold futures for December delivery dropped 0.3% to $3,987.50 at that time. Spot gold was at $3,977.99 by 0506 GMT, still down 0.2%, and December futures had dropped 0.4% to $3,983.20. The dollar climbed to a 19-month high against major currencies, making dollar-denominated gold more expensive for buyers using other currencies.

A later Reuters report said gold prices fell for a third straight session on Thursday. Spot gold was down 1% to $3,948.82 per ounce by 1114 GMT, having touched its lowest level since November 4 earlier in the session. US gold futures for December delivery fell 1.1% to $3,954.50 per ounce.

The selling continued into the close. December gold futures settled at $3,885.30 on Thursday, about 27% below their record close in January, according to Yahoo Finance data cited by CoinMarketCap, which said gold closed at a one-year low. Gold futures fell 2.88% to $3,885, including post-market trading, after dropping as low as $3,857, their worst day since October 30 and their lowest close since early October 2025. That move took out early-November lows below the $4,000 line.

The DXY dollar index rose 0.33% to 103.19 on Thursday, another highest close since April 7, 2025. The 2-year Treasury yield rose 13.8 basis points to 4.88%, a two-day gain of 31.8 basis points that was the biggest since March 2023 and the highest close since September 29. The 10-year yield rose 13.3 basis points to 5.13%, its biggest gain since September 23 and its highest close since October 21, taking it back above the 5% level. The 30-year yield rose 10 basis points to 5.46%, its biggest gain since May 15 and its highest close since October 19, as the 30-year auction tailed on weaker demand.

Although gold is typically seen as an inflation hedge, higher interest rates increase the opportunity cost of holding non-yielding bullion, reducing its appeal to investors. A stronger dollar also puts pressure on gold as it becomes more expensive for buyers in other currencies. The S&P 500 fell 0.35% to 7,592 on Thursday, and the Nasdaq Composite fell 0.39% to 25,366. The Street said the S&P 500 opened at its low of the day, 7,551, below Wednesday's close of 7,572, ending a run of four straight higher lows, and traded as high as 7,653 before the close. The Nasdaq Composite dropped as much as 1.6% to 25,068, below its 200-day moving average of 25,130, before recovering to close back above that line. The Russell 2000 fell 1.66% to 2,799, its worst day in a week. The 2-year yield finished about 101 basis points above the midpoint of the federal funds target range, 3.875%, pricing in a few more rate hikes, according to The Street.

Ilya Spivak, head of global macro at Tastylive, said, "The (gold) market has had a meaningful pullback over the past several days in the wake of that US inflation data and the rise in rate hike expectations around that but I think the markets are pointedly struggling for a follow through." He added, "We're kind of sitting on a critical level here, basically just around the $4,000 mark." Those comments were reported when spot gold was at $3,981.49, before futures settled well below $4,000.

Ole Hansen, head of commodity strategy at Saxo Bank, said gold retreated to test key support around $3,970, as sellers focused on persistent US inflation and hawkish comments from several Fed officials. He said that following the hotter-than-expected CPI readings, speculation has grown that the FOMC could opt for a 50-basis-point rate hike at its December meeting, despite the recent sharp decline in energy prices that may help ease inflation pressures in the months ahead.

According to CME's FedWatch tool, markets saw a 91% chance of a 25-basis-point rate hike in December, while the probability of a larger 50-basis-point move rose to 10% from zero before the inflation data. The same tool showed that investors increased their bets on a potential half-percentage-point interest rate increase in December and that market pricing indicated expectations of another hike in March 2027.

OCBC said in a note, "We continue to see two-way risks in the near term, with oil prices, yields and US dollar remaining important drivers. While the macro backdrop has become less favorable for gold, we are not turning bearish on its medium-term prospects." WTI futures rose 2.55% to $88.74 on Thursday, their highest close since November 6, reversing an early drop to $85.13 after President Trump said the United States does not need a deal with Iran. Copper futures fell 1.49% to $6.20, their lowest close since July 8, according to The Street. That one-day rise came as Hansen pointed to a recent sharp decline in energy prices that he said may help ease inflation pressures in the months ahead.

The dollar's strength held on Friday. CoinMarketCap said the US dollar index traded at its highest level since April 2025 on Friday, and the 10-year Treasury yield climbed back above 5.1%, as traders priced another Fed rate hike. Producer prices rose 6.5% in the year to October, the largest rise since November 2022, the Bureau of Labor Statistics reported Friday. Bitcoin has fallen about 9% since the Nov. 6 close, further than gold futures, and traded at $68,542 at 12:05 p.m. ET on Friday after touching $67,653 overnight, its lowest since Aug. 19, CoinMarketCap data showed.

The Nasdaq Composite is still up for the week, even after it fell on Friday, CoinMarketCap said. QCP Capital named Japan's response to the yen near 160 per dollar as the next test for the dollar's rally. The yen weakened past 160 per dollar on Thursday, and Tokyo warned against one-sided moves, QCP noted. The firm framed the next turn as whether Tokyo steps in before the Fed raises rates again.

Among other metals, spot palladium climbed 1% to $1,037.25 per ounce in earlier Thursday trading. A later report said spot silver fell 1% to $51.16 per ounce and spot palladium dropped 0.6% to $1,021.25 after hitting its highest level since October 22 on Wednesday.

The scheduled policy decision in view is the FOMC meeting in December.

Law & Courts

South Carolina judge denies bond for father after infant found buried in dog crate

A Lexington County judge on Nov. 12 denied bond on all counts for Michael Comer, the 25-year-old South Carolina father charged after authorities said his 11-month-old son, Koah Sharp, was found buried in a dog crate. Comer will remain at the Lexington County Detention Center. Warrants cited by The Post and Courier charge Comer with attempted murder, unlawful conduct toward a child, custodial interference and burglary, the same four charges listed by the Lexington County Sheriff's Department.

Koah had been in the care of his paternal grandmother, Patricia Comer, on Nov. 9, Sheriff Jay Koon said. She left to run an errand and left the boy with Comer until another caregiver arrived. "By the time the caregiver got there, Michael was gone and Koah was gone with him," Koon said.

Family members filed a missing persons report that afternoon, Gray News reported. Patricia Comer told CNN affiliate WACH that she called 911. "I had a bad feeling that something was not right," she said.

Koon said deputies went to the nearby home of Comer's father, on Parthenon Road in Batesburg-Leesville, and found both doors open and baby belongings inside. Deputies were called there around lunchtime on Nov. 9, The Post and Courier reported. Video from businesses in the area showed Comer without the baby on the afternoon of Nov. 9, Koon said.

Newsweek reported that he was arrested that night while hiding in an outbuilding in Batesburg-Leesville, and investigators said the circumstances of the arrest led to the burglary charge. Koah was not with him.

The search continued on Nov. 10, when, CNN reported, a search party working near the grandfather's house around 1:30 p.m. found freshly disturbed dirt about 50 yards into the woods. As investigators began digging, they heard the baby crying. "As you can imagine, the race was on. Everyone was trying to get him out," Koon said.

CNN said nearly 100 officers took part; Newsweek said more than 100 law enforcement and public safety personnel were involved. Koah was found about a foot underground in a 2-foot-by-2-foot dog crate wrapped in a towel, Koon told CNN. Koon said the towel created an air pocket and that cool temperatures also likely helped the boy survive; CNN said the area reached as low as 40 degrees Fahrenheit from Monday night into Tuesday morning. "By the grace of God, [it] probably provided enough air for him to survive an estimated 24 hours under that dirt," Koon said, according to Gray News reporting carried by KPTV. Those reports also said searchers noted pine straw at the site.

He estimated Koah had been underground about 24 hours. The sheriff told reporters the boy was "clinging to life" when he was freed. Koah was released from a hospital Tuesday night, Nov. 10, and is expected to make a full recovery, Koon and the sheriff's office said.

Newsweek, citing South Carolina court records reviewed by FITSNews, reported that on October 8 Comer pleaded guilty before Circuit Court Judge Jane H. Merrill to aggravated breach of peace, a reduced charge from a July 22 arrest for first-degree assault and battery. In a separate case after a May 13 arrest, he pleaded guilty to third-degree domestic violence, reduced from a second-degree charge. Newsweek reported that he received a youthful offender sentence of up to five years, suspended after 78 days already served, followed by 13 months of probation. The Associated Press, citing court records, reported that the domestic violence case involved Koah's mother and that in May he pushed her from behind while she was holding Koah, causing the baby to hit a clothes dryer.

At the Nov. 12 bond hearing, Comer was not visible to the small audience and said few words, The Post and Courier reported. Tyler Douglas, lead investigator for the Lexington County Sheriff's Department, told Judge Gary W. Reinhart that Comer showed no remorse for attempting to kill his son and tried to flee the state. An attorney had not yet been appointed, the Associated Press reported, even as Reinhart approved a public defender.

Authorities have not suggested a motive. John Comer, the child's grandfather, told the Associated Press that it "just seems like he already had this planned out, and he knew what he was doing." Koah's great-aunt, Amber Hammond, told the Associated Press that her nephew had talked about taking the baby to New York because "he didn't want to be in the same state as the mom," and that when Koah vanished, she and others assumed Comer had given the boy to someone.

In a 911 call released Thursday, Patricia Comer told a dispatcher that her son had "severe schizophrenia and bipolar and depression," that she had been "kindly worried" about leaving Koah with him that morning, and that "He threatened to kill himself last night and asked me for some sleeping pills," the Associated Press reported. She also said he had been making comments about going to New York, which she said he could not do because he was on probation.

John Comer offered a contrasting description of the same morning. He said his son seemed perfectly rational when he picked up Michael Comer and Koah, and that Michael Comer told him he had a job and a plan to care for the child. "He was not emotional," John Comer said outside the law enforcement complex Thursday, adding that his son "appeared a loving, a loving and caring father" and "was in his right mind."

Koah's mother, Dezarae Sharp, also disputed an account of the family's situation. Newsweek reported that in a Facebook post she said Koah was doing well and staying with a family friend while she was hospitalized for a pregnancy-related condition, and that she said her son had not been taken from her custody. The Post and Courier reported that the child's mother was in the hospital the morning of Nov. 12.

Koon said the parents "have had some situations" and that children "sometimes get caught in the middle." CNN reported that he said "there were obviously some issues" recently between Comer and Koah's mother.

Comer remains in custody after the Nov. 12 denial of bond. Reinhart told him he has the right to a preliminary hearing and said, "That's where the state must show probable cause that you committed the offenses you're being charged with." The judge also said, "That's not an automatic hearing anymore."

The next court date is Dec. 10. Newsweek reported that it was unclear whether the arrest would lead to probation-violation proceedings.

Global

EU leaders agree on a European team after Ukraine talks in Abu Dhabi

Russia and Ukraine held their first U.S.-mediated talks in nine months on Tuesday and Wednesday in Abu Dhabi. The session, the first since February, overlapped with a Group of Seven foreign ministers' meeting in Paris, where European diplomats urged U.S. Secretary of State Marco Rubio to ensure that European nations have a direct role in any settlement of the war that began with Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine. The Paris gathering was Rubio's first with allies since last week's midterm elections. The Associated Press reported that President Donald Trump must reckon with Democrats taking over the U.S. Congress in January.

Trump last week declared that a grand deal about European security was in the works, describing it as "a big agreement" that is "going to involve more than Ukraine." Ukrainian Foreign Minister Andriy Sybiga said "no one has the right to decide our fate behind our backs." German Foreign Minister Johann Wadephul, after talks with Rubio, said "It is certainly neither necessary, nor right, nor appropriate to make a fundamental change" to Europe's security architecture, and added, "Europe belongs at this table. There can be no solution without Europe - only with Europe."

Neither Rubio nor Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy attended the talks. The United States was represented by Trump's son-in-law Jared Kushner and envoy Steve Witkoff. Ukraine's delegation was led by Rustem Umerov, head of the Foreign Intelligence Service, and Russia's by Vladimir Medinsky, a Putin aide who led Moscow's team in Istanbul in March 2022. The first day's session lasted four hours, with another held Wednesday, and European officials did not take part in the main three-way discussions.

Rubio, speaking in Paris, said "It looks like the talks in Abu Dhabi were very productive," but also said, "It's not like something that's on the verge of being solved in the next 24 hours or anything like that." Zelenskyy called the session "constructive and quite substantive" and said it was largely about humanitarian issues, including prisoner exchanges, returning civilians and children, and security guarantees. Kushner said both countries "shared specific ideas to narrow the gaps and find a path to end the war," and that a new prisoner exchange deal, the largest of the war to date according to Kushner, "will reunite families, build trust, and create momentum toward peace." He also said "significant differences remain."

On Thursday, Witkoff and Kushner met Russian President Vladimir Putin in Moscow, then traveled to Dublin to brief Zelenskyy, who was there for a meeting of the European Political Community. After the Kremlin meeting, Witkoff told reporters, "I think progress was made," and "We've restarted it. We've set a new meeting." He said a European representative would be present for the next stage of talks, adding, "They're our partners, and it's important for them to be involved." Zelenskyy said "There really is this window of opportunity, when all the participants involved in this negotiating process could get the result by the beginning of 2027."

French President Emmanuel Macron said in Dublin, "Without Europeans, there is no support for Ukraine," and "There is no more U.S. support." "At the end of the day, we will be the ones to define and decide for our future security as Europeans," he said. The Hindu reported that Europeans do not trust Trump to negotiate on their behalf after a series of tense standoffs over trade, Greenland, and conflicts in Iran and Ukraine. On Friday, EU leaders at an informal summit in Dublin agreed that Europe will be represented by the EU institutions together with France, Germany, the United Kingdom, Italy, and Poland, rather than a single envoy. "Today, the leaders agreed that our European team for the negotiations on peace in Ukraine is the European Union institutions and the E5 of the 'Coalition of the Willing'," European Council President António Costa told reporters.

Costa said the EU will take the lead on Ukraine's "European future, its reconstruction, and prosperity," while security guarantees "must be addressed within the framework of the coalition of the willing, in close coordination with Nato." A senior diplomat called the existing set-up "not ideal" and said EU capitals had supported sending EU foreign policy chief Kaja Kallas but that it was "just not possible," leaving open whether she will take a seat. Meduza, citing Bloomberg, reported that national security advisers from the EU, Germany, France, the United Kingdom, Italy, and Poland will represent Europe at the technical level, and that Europe has yet to appoint representatives for negotiations at the highest level.

U.S. and Ukrainian officials have said Europe will join the next round. Meduza reported that after the Moscow meeting, Witkoff said the talks would be expanded to include Europe, that Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov said Moscow had no objection, and that Russia's Foreign Ministry said the subject had not come up during Putin's meeting with the Americans. Peskov said "a concrete decision on broadening the talks" could be made once the Kremlin "understands [the Europeans'] true intentions and willingness to help advance the peace process." Kushner has said a "comprehensive and lasting settlement will also require direct engagement between Russia and our European partners on the broader security arrangements that affect them."

The two governments remain far apart on the terms of any deal. Putin wants Ukraine to pull its troops back from four regions that Moscow annexed in September 2022 but has never fully captured, and demands that Ukraine abandon its bid to join NATO and accept strict limits on its military, and has demanded that NATO troops be pulled back from Russia's borders. Kyiv has rejected demands to surrender the entirety of the eastern Donbas. Zelenskyy rejects limits on Ukraine's military or its arrangements with Western partners, and Ukraine argues an immediate ceasefire is the necessary first step toward a wider settlement; Putin has rejected a general halt in hostilities and told the U.S. envoys that foreign peacekeeping troops stationed in Ukraine would be considered a "continuation of war."

A new meeting has been set, Witkoff said. Yuri Ushakov, Putin's foreign policy adviser, told reporters after the Moscow meeting that Putin will continue talks on Ukraine with Trump at the Asian-Pacific Economic Cooperation summit in China later this month. European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen said the Commission is preparing its 22nd package of sanctions against Russia, alongside a proposal to cover Ukraine's financial needs in 2027, and "will come with the sanctions package and the proposal for the financial needs before the next European Council," which is scheduled to take place in Brussels across Nov. 26-27.

Energy & Climate

Guterres calls for taxes on energy profits at COP31

The 31st U.N. climate summit, COP31, is being held in Antalya, Turkey, from November 9 to 20 and is being jointly presided over by Turkey and Australia. A two-day meeting of global leaders opened on Wednesday, November 11, alongside the negotiations. Leaders and diplomats are aiming to reinvigorate efforts on reducing emissions, delivering climate finance and the transition away from fossil fuels.

Greenhouse gas emissions caused by human activity have led the planet's temperature to rise 1.47 degrees Celsius above the average of preindustrial times for the first nine months of 2026, according to the U.N.'s World Meteorological Organization. United Nations Secretary-General António Guterres told the leaders' meeting that exceeding the Paris Agreement's 1.5-degree Celsius threshold was now inevitable, but that governments could still limit the severity and duration of the overshoot. He said greenhouse gas emissions reached record levels in 2025.

Climate Action Tracker found on Thursday that current policies are set to heat the planet by 2.7C by the end of the century. Guterres said national climate plans would leave the world facing more than 2.5°C of warming by the end of the century, even if fully implemented. The US has no official representation for a second year, and top leaders from China, India, Germany, and Canada were also absent. Andy Burnham, the British prime minister, was the only G7 leader to attend the talks.

On Wednesday at the opening ceremony of the COP31 Leaders' Summit, Guterres called on governments to tax windfall profits made by oil companies and channel the proceeds into climate recovery efforts in vulnerable countries. He also proposed a new Climate Justice Compact. In those remarks, and again in a Thursday interview with Bloomberg Television at the summit, he said a group of nine major producers, which he also described as the nine largest oil companies, had reported collective profits of $95 billion in the last quarter.

The Fund for Responding to Loss and Damage currently stands at only $600 million, according to Guterres, who said, "This looks like a joke." He also said the fund had received about $600 million, equivalent to what the companies made in about 14 hours. "It's time for governments to tax these windfall profits - and use them to help vulnerable countries recover," he said. "Polluters must pay," he said.

In the Thursday interview, he said energy sector profits that swelled alongside global conflicts are "morally unjustifiable" and should face additional taxes to fund climate measures. "They are making these profits with an activity that is causing severe damage to the global environment and severe damage in the acceleration of climate change," he said. Nations have existing mechanisms "able to tax windfall profits - it's a political decision, it's not a technical problem," he said. "There's something fundamentally wrong," he said in the Thursday interview. The Los Angeles Times reported that he has held discussions with governments about an additional levy on oil and gas companies, and that he said, "Those taxes should be used to alleviate the situation of those that are suffering."

He said emerging and developing economies outside China were home to nearly two-thirds of the world's population but received less than a fifth of global clean energy investment. "If they cannot access affordable clean energy, fossil fuels will fill the gap - locking in emissions for decades," he said. On finance, he said developed countries must lead in delivering at least $300 billion a year by 2035, while setting out concrete plans to at least triple adaptation finance by then, and called for the $1.3 trillion climate finance goal to translate into funding flowing to developing countries.

"Climate justice is the defining challenge of the next decade," he said. "We cannot demand that countries transform their economies while denying them the means to do so." He called for an end to new coal and oil and gas infrastructure that would make the temperature goal harder to achieve. UN Climate Change Executive Secretary Simon Stiell identified electrification, tripling adaptation finance, and improving access to climate funding as key priorities for COP31.

"Action is not yet fast enough - the science demands we drastically pace up," Stiell said. Chris Bowen, COP31 President of Negotiations, said only 0.2 per cent of adaptation finance reached small island developing states. On the fourth day of the two-week summit, Greta Thunberg, 23, made her first appearance at the annual U.N. climate talks in five years. "The planet is not dying, it is being killed," she said.

Gita Chaudhary, Nepal's environment minister, recounted a landslide caused by a melting glacier that struck the country in August, claiming more than 1,500 lives. Nepal's foreign minister, Shisir Khanal, attended the Antalya Summit on November 11 and met Guterres on the sidelines. The Kathmandu Post reported that Khanal returned home on Saturday, and that Chaudhary will lead the Nepali delegation for the remaining days as the conference continues through November 20.

The account of the Thursday interview said many governments are reluctant to act on company profits. "The power of the oil and gas lobby is very high," Guterres said. European fossil fuel producers faced additional levies after Russia's invasion of Ukraine. The U.K. announced in May it would close a loophole that would prevent overseas oil and gas firms from paying little or no corporation tax in the country, and Poland's government has imposed a further tax on producers of liquid fuel.

The European Commission is analyzing the potential effects of imposing a tax on windfall energy profits. It would be a "good idea" to use any such levy to finance climate adaptation measures, said Teresa Ribera, the EU's antitrust chief. "If this isn't the moment for governments to act, when will it be," said Fanny Petitbon, a policy analyst with environmental nonprofit 350.org.

Campaigners have criticised governments for their slow pace of emissions cuts and for, so far, failing to rally around a phaseout of fossil fuels in the negotiations. Some countries want an electrification agreement, one of the main potential promises expected at the talks, to specify that the electricity will be derived from clean energy sources. Guterres said governments cannot continue expanding fossil fuel production while claiming to be committed to limiting global warming to 1.5°C. "We have a choice: Either plan this just transition - or pay for it in chaos and lost livelihoods," he said.

COP31 is scheduled to continue through November 20, with negotiations in the remaining days focusing on emissions reductions, climate resilience and implementation of earlier commitments. An electrification goal remains one of the main potential promises expected at the talks.

Level Up

Retrieval-augmented generation: looking up sources before a model answers

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) is a technique that enables large language models (LLMs) to retrieve and incorporate new information from external data sources. With RAG, LLMs first refer to a specified set of documents, then respond to user queries. Those documents supplement the LLM's pre-existing training data, so the models can use domain-specific or updated information that is not available in the training data.

Wikipedia says the term was introduced in a 2020 paper that described combining a parametric language model with a non-parametric external memory accessed through retrieval at inference time. A 2020 NeurIPS paper by Lewis PSH, Perez E et al is titled "Retrieval-augmented generation for knowledge-intensive NLP tasks." Penghao Zhao, Hailin Zhang, Qinhan Yu and colleagues, in a survey whose version of record is 06 January 2026, write that RAG enhances generation by retrieving relevant objects from available data stores. They write that AI-generated content still faces hurdles such as updating knowledge, handling long-tail data, mitigating data leakage, and managing high training and inference costs, and that RAG has recently emerged as a paradigm to address such challenges.

LLMs can provide incorrect information when they rely on static training data. Wikipedia records that when Google first demonstrated its LLM tool "Bard" (later re-branded to Google Gemini), the LLM provided incorrect information about the James Webb Space Telescope, and that this error contributed to a $100 billion decline in Google's stock value. RAG pulls relevant text from databases, uploaded documents, or web sources, and it reduces the need to retrain LLMs with new data.

Wikipedia quotes Ars Technica as saying, "RAG is a way of improving LLM performance, in essence by blending the LLM process with a web search or other document look-up process to help LLMs stick to the facts." IBM, quoted on the same page, states that "in the generative phase, the LLM draws from the augmented prompt and its internal representation of its training data to synthesize" an answer. RAG also allows LLMs to include sources in their responses, so users can verify the cited sources.

In a typical pipeline, data is converted into LLM embeddings, numerical representations in a large vector space, and stored in a vector database. A document retriever selects the most relevant documents used to augment the query, and the model feeds that information into the LLM through prompt engineering of the user's original query, a step that has been called "prompt stuffing." RAG can be used on unstructured (usually text), semi-structured, or structured data, for example knowledge graphs.

Zhao, Hailin Zhang, Qinhan Yu and colleagues group retrieval methods into sparse retrieval, which uses term matching metrics such as TF-IDF and BM25 with an inverted index, and dense retrieval, which represents queries and keys as dense embedding vectors, often with BERT in an approach called Dense Passage Retrieval (DPR), and builds an Approximate Nearest Neighbor (ANN) index. In the common query-based form, retrieved content is merged with the user's original query, as when Lewis et al. leveraged DPR and employed BART as the generator.

Cloud vendors have packaged the pipeline as a managed service. Future Market Insights says Google made Vertex AI RAG Engine generally available on 9 January 2025, AWS did the same for GraphRAG in Amazon Bedrock Knowledge Bases on 7 March 2025, and Microsoft introduced agentic retrieval in Azure AI Search as a public preview on 19 May 2025.

Amazon Web Services describes a production example at Hathr.AI, which integrates a HIPAA-aligned tool with Amazon Bedrock in AWS GovCloud (US), isolated AWS Regions with FedRAMP High authorization. To make each upload searchable, Hathr.AI splits the document into chunks, converts each chunk into a vector embedding, and writes those embeddings to the customer's vector index. After moving vector search to Amazon S3 Vectors, AWS says Hathr.AI has served vector queries with zero failed requests at sub-100 ms average latency.

With that latency, AWS says an agent can run several searches per question and still stay within Hathr.AI's latency budget, so the team moved from one retrieval per question to an agentic pattern built on Strands Agents. The foundation model reads the question, calls a search tool, inspects the results, and either answers or searches again. S3 Vectors holds embeddings in one index per customer, and Amazon DocumentDB holds the text chunks those embeddings point to. Tenant isolation does not rely on a metadata filter: Hathr.AI's server picks the customer index from the authenticated session.

What the method can and cannot do is disputed. Wikipedia says this method helps reduce AI hallucinations. It also says hallucinations have caused chatbots to describe policies that do not exist, or to recommend nonexistent legal cases to lawyers looking for citations. Ars Technica, quoted there, also says "It is not a direct solution because the LLM can still hallucinate around the source material in its response." Wikipedia adds that LLMs can generate misinformation even when pulling from factually correct sources if they misinterpret the context, and cites MIT Technology Review's example of a response stating, "The United States has had one Muslim president, Barack Hussein Obama," after a model retrieved a rhetorical chapter title.

IBM, as summarized by Wikipedia, has pointed to another gap: without specific training, models may generate answers even when they should indicate uncertainty, which can arise when the model lacks the ability to assess its own knowledge limitations. Zhao, Hailin Zhang, Qinhan Yu and colleagues write that information retrieval is inherently flawed due to information loss in item representations and ANN search, and that the impact of retrieval noise remains unclear, because recent research also finds that noisy retrieval results might enhance generation quality. They add that retrieval incurs non-negligible overhead in most cases and increases latency.

The same survey notes a recent notion that "long-context models like Gemini 1.5 will replace RAG," and says this assertion overlooks RAG's flexibility in managing dynamic information, encompassing both up-to-date and long-tail knowledge. The authors expect RAG to benefit from long context generation, rather than being replaced by it. A study they cite found that using top-k rather than a single retrieval improves attribution but harms fluency in query-based RAG.

The scheduled item to watch is a legal grace period. Future Market Insights says South Korea's AI Basic Act took effect on 22 January 2026, and that the Ministry of Science and ICT announced a grace period of at least one year and a support desk to help businesses prepare. FMI also says the legislation does not classify every RAG application as a high-impact use. No later decision date for the end of that grace period is given in the cited sources.
