Politics & Elections

After the midterm wave, both parties reorganize for divided government

Tuesday's midterm elections ended unified Republican control of Washington. Democrats won majorities in both the House and the Senate, setting up two years of divided government for the rest of President Trump's second term. Since then, both parties have begun choosing new leaders, the White House has replaced its chief of staff, and two Senate races remain uncalled.

Republicans went into the election with a 53-47 Senate majority, Roll Call reported. CBS News projected Wednesday that Democrats will control at least 54 Senate seats, and Roll Call put the likely range at 54 to 56 depending on the two uncalled races. In the House, Democrats had won more than 250 seats by Friday, well above the 218 needed, according to Associated Press calls reported by Anadolu Agency; CBS News estimated a final total of 251 to 268.

In North Carolina, the AP had called nine of the state's 14 House seats for Democrats as of Thursday, compared with four they held going in, NPR and The Washington Post reported. Republican losses included the seat of Rep. Richard Hudson, chairman of the National Republican Congressional Committee.

The most immediate change came in the House Republican leadership. Speaker Mike Johnson told his staff Wednesday that he will not run for minority leader in the new Congress, according to CBS News, Axios and Roll Call. In a letter to colleagues, Johnson cited the toll on his family after three years as speaker.

Majority Leader Steve Scalise of Louisiana and Judiciary Committee Chairman Jim Jordan of Ohio both announced bids for minority leader. Both sought the speakership in 2023, Axios noted. The winner needs a majority of House Republicans in a secret ballot.

The two campaigns are making different cases. Jordan wrote to colleagues that he is "battle-tested on the oversight front" and that the old approach "will not cut it against an emboldened and energetic Democrat majority," Roll Call and Axios reported. A person familiar with Scalise's plans described him as "a proven and tested leader who is ready on day one," and said his top goal is to lead the conference back into the majority.

In the Senate, a spokesperson said Majority Leader John Thune will run for minority leader, according to Roll Call and The Washington Post, and neither outlet reported any challenger. Thune wrote that Senate Republicans "will not stand idly by" as Democrats try to undo their record.

On the Democratic side, Minority Leader Chuck Schumer predicted Wednesday, "I'll be reelected as majority leader," according to Roll Call. The Hill and The Washington Post reported that he is expected to win when Senate Democrats hold their leadership elections, though The Hill said some senators-elect, including Illinois' Juliana Stratton, have said they will not back him. In the House, Democratic Leader Hakeem Jeffries faces no opposition to become speaker, NBC News reported. He would be the first Black speaker, with the floor vote set for Jan. 3.

The White House also made a change. On Friday, Trump named Deputy Chief of Staff Stephen Miller as chief of staff, replacing Susie Wiles, who will move to Trump's political operation, NBC News and NPR reported. Miller takes over Monday. "Stephen's been with me since the beginning. He gets it," Trump told reporters, according to NPR.

Two Senate races remain uncalled. In Mississippi, Democrat Scott Colom led Republican Sen. Cindy Hyde-Smith by more than 2,000 votes as of Friday afternoon, NPR reported, and Mississippi Today put the gap near 2,500 out of almost 1 million votes cast. The state has no recount process, and counties run their own elections.

The size of the remaining Mississippi vote is unclear. The Colom campaign said it knew of more than 15,000 affidavit ballots, a form of provisional ballot, while the state Republican Party chairman estimated 20,000 outstanding ballots, NPR reported. Secretary of State Michael Watson has estimated 12,000 to 20,000 affidavit ballots, WLBT reported. Mailed ballots postmarked by Election Day can count if they arrive by Nov. 10.

In Alaska, Democrat Mary Peltola led Republican Sen. Dan Sullivan by 637 first-choice votes, 102,962 to 102,325, in results posted Wednesday, Alaska's News Source reported. About a quarter of ballots were still outstanding. Ranked-choice tabulation begins Nov. 18, according to the station and Al Jazeera.

The parties disagree about why the election went the way it did. Vice President JD Vance wrote Wednesday that "the reason we lost is the war," referring to the conflict with Iran, the BBC and ABC News reported. Trump called that "largely true" but said he would "do it again" because Iran cannot have a nuclear weapon.

Others in the party pointed elsewhere. A White House official blamed Republican "candidate quality," according to RTÉ and Scripps News. An ABC News voter poll found that 34% of House voters called the Iran war "crucial" to their vote, compared with 49% for the cost of living and 55% for government corruption.

The two sides also disagree about Miller's promotion. Jeffries called for Miller to be fired, and Schumer wrote that Trump "learned nothing from his historic defeat," NBC News and NPR reported. Miller wrote that his mission is to "fight with all of our soul to support our President and defend this magnificent Republic."

With majorities in both chambers, Democrats will gain subpoena power in January. NPR reported that likely early subjects include the Trump family's finances and the Justice Department's handling of the Jeffrey Epstein files. Jordan has pledged to push back on what he called Democratic efforts at "investigating every facet of the Administration," according to Politico, and Roll Call reported that Republicans are preparing to defend the president.

Several dates are already set. Lawmakers return next week for a lame-duck session in which Republicans still control both chambers, Politico and The Hill reported. House and Senate Republicans hold leadership elections Tuesday, Nov. 10, which is also Mississippi's deadline for mailed ballots to arrive. Mississippi counties must certify results by Nov. 13, and Alaska's ranked-choice tabulation is set for Nov. 18.

Further out, Congress must pass a funding bill by mid-December to avoid a government shutdown and must also finish the annual defense policy bill, Politico and The Hill reported. Senate Democrats hold their leadership elections in early December, according to The Hill. The new Congress convenes Jan. 3, when the House will vote for speaker.

AI

Update: OpenAI proposes public rules for AI campuses after voters demand limits

OpenAI published a National Community Compact and a Compute Campus Policy Blueprint on November 5, two days after voters opposed data-center expansion in communities across the United States. The documents combine promises for future campuses the company owns and operates with proposals for government rules across the industry. OpenAI says it will cover its electricity and infrastructure costs, disclose local impacts and limit confidentiality agreements with public officials.

The distinction between a corporate commitment and a public rule runs through the announcement. OpenAI's compact describes what the company promises at its own future facilities; its blueprint asks policymakers to establish protections regardless of the developer. CNBC reported that the company encouraged lawmakers to turn similar commitments into laws. Neither publication of the compact nor the election results, by themselves, enact those proposed requirements.

The immediate background was Tuesday's voting. CNBC reported that voters supported restrictions or rejected pro-development positions in all but one of 41 local ballot measures across 10 states, based on Ballotpedia's analysis and additional results the network gathered. TechCrunch independently described opposition across several states, including Ohio, and reported that concerns included utility bills, environmental effects and residents' ability to influence development. Those results concern particular local measures, not a nationwide prohibition on AI infrastructure.

OpenAI presents its response as a continuation of work rather than a policy assembled after the election. Its announcement points to community commitments begun in January and subsequent agreements in Georgia and Ohio. Chief Global Affairs Officer Chris Lehane told CNBC that the blueprint had been in development since August. The timing of publication followed the voting, but the company's account places its preparation earlier.

One prominent change concerns secrecy. OpenAI says it will not use nondisclosure agreements with public officials, while retaining narrow protections for legitimate security information, trade secrets and proprietary or competitively sensitive material. The Verge reported both the commitment and its exceptions, against a background of scrutiny over confidential discussions between developers and local officials. The pledge is therefore not a promise to make every commercial or security detail public.

That qualification matters to understanding what was announced. Officials are not being promised unrestricted access to every document, and the public is not being promised publication of every negotiation. Instead, OpenAI is committing to avoid general confidentiality requirements while preserving specified categories of protected information. Its separate proposal for industry rules also calls for narrow limits on when developers can require officials to sign such agreements.

Electricity is another central provision. OpenAI says it will pay the full cost of the power it uses and the infrastructure needed to serve it; The Verge and CNBC both reported that commitment. The company also promises financial assurances before major spending begins so other customers are protected if a project is delayed, reduced or canceled. The compact describes these as protections for surrounding customers, not merely a statement that the campus will pay its monthly electricity bill.

The blueprint asks state utility commissions to apply that principle more broadly by requiring large customers to cover the costs they add to the grid. OpenAI also supports easier connections for campuses that can safely reduce demand during shortages. These are the company's policy proposals, not descriptions of a single new national tariff or an enacted nationwide cost-allocation system. CNBC's reporting similarly distinguishes OpenAI's own pledges from its request for legislation.

Public reporting is intended to make other effects more visible. OpenAI promises campus-specific water plans, priority for closed-loop or low-water cooling, and publication of site-level electricity and water use. It also promises construction plans and independent noise studies before operations begin, with subsequent annual reporting. These provisions accompany the broader disclosure and community-input commitments described by CNBC; they do not amount to a pledge that campuses will consume no water or produce no noise.

The compact includes economic commitments as well. OpenAI says it will publish expected jobs, work with unions and training providers, and report on hiring and apprenticeships. The Verge reported its promise to publish job expectations and hiring information. The company also says each future campus will have a local compact reflecting local priorities, with residents engaged early enough to influence plans and given a continuing point of contact.

The disagreement is about whether building more capacity can proceed on terms communities accept. OpenAI argues that additional American computing infrastructure can lower the cost of delivering AI, broaden access and support national competitiveness. TechCrunch reported a contrasting emphasis from Color of Change president Nadine Smith: companies should pay for their power, meet clean-air standards, disclose resource use and obtain neighbors' consent. Her argument concerns conditions and accountability, rather than accepting infrastructure investment alone as sufficient justification.

Those positions overlap on disclosure and protecting households from costs, but they are not identical. OpenAI proposes continued development with company commitments and public safeguards. TechCrunch also reported demands for moratoriums and disagreement even among Democrats over a blanket freeze, including concerns about AI progress and national security. The reporting does not establish a common position among all opponents or show that the new compact has resolved their objections.

The announcement supplies some recurring checkpoints, but not a dated timetable for adoption of the proposed laws. OpenAI commits to reporting progress annually and to publishing noise studies before operations and annually afterward. Those are stated reporting intervals, not evidence that a particular campus has already fulfilled them. No fixed legislative vote or compact-wide implementation deadline is identified in the announcement or CNBC's account.

For now, the confirmed development is a published set of corporate promises and a request for wider rules. Its scope remains future campuses OpenAI owns and operates, while the policy blueprint addresses the industry more generally. The unresolved question is not whether the company has announced protections, but how the promised disclosures, financial assurances and local commitments will be put into effect at individual sites.

Tech

Starlink’s mobile ambitions move from satellites to cell sites

SpaceX’s plan to become a nationwide mobile carrier acquired a concrete terrestrial component this week. On November 4, the company and Qualcomm announced a multiyear agreement to supply technology for Starlink Mobile’s ground-based cellular network. The planned deployment covers tens of thousands of sites, beginning in 2027, according to reporting by CNBC, Axios, The Verge and RCR Wireless News.

The development is not the launch of a nationwide phone service. It is a hardware-and-software agreement for building the infrastructure behind one. That distinction separates what the companies have announced from what remains unproven: whether the resulting network can match established carriers on coverage, capacity and customer experience.

Starlink is already a satellite broadband business, but providing internet through a dedicated terminal and operating a mass-market smartphone network are different undertakings. CNBC reported that SpaceX’s current direct-to-device satellite services cannot supply the full voice, video and data coverage available from established smartphone carriers. Axios likewise reported that satellites cannot substitute for terrestrial capacity wherever large numbers of phone users congregate.

The proposed system combines those two approaches rather than replacing conventional cellular infrastructure with satellites. Ground-based equipment would provide cellular service alongside Starlink’s direct-to-device constellation. CNBC and RCR describe the terrestrial network as a complement to satellite connectivity, while The Verge reports that combining the two is central to the company’s mobile plans.

Qualcomm’s contribution is its Dragonwing cellular infrastructure platform, comprising chips and software for the radio and baseband functions inside base stations. Axios reported that Qualcomm will supply the chips and foundational processing software while SpaceX develops additional software and other network elements. The arrangement therefore involves technology for operating a network, not simply the modem inside a subscriber’s phone.

That also gives the agreement a different role for Qualcomm. CNBC describes a company better known for components in devices than for the base stations serving them, although it already works in cellular infrastructure. RCR identifies the Starlink project as an opportunity to expand its radio-access-network business; Qualcomm said the agreement is its largest 5G initiative to date, CNBC reported.

The basic architecture is conventional enough to carry conventional construction obligations. Axios reported that the agreement points toward traditional high-powered cellular infrastructure, rather than primarily small cellular stations attached to Starlink broadband terminals. CNBC reported that SpaceX had not announced tower-company partners or specified how many entirely new towers would be necessary.

A cell site is not necessarily a new tower. CNBC notes that carriers commonly lease space on structures that host multiple networks. Consequently, the announced site count describes the intended scale of equipment deployment, not a confirmed count of new structures, signed leases or completed installations. RCR also reported that SpaceX had not disclosed how many sites would be deployed in each phase.

The companies’ case is that integration can make the two networks work together. RCR quoted Starlink executive Michael Nicolls describing an intended nationwide satellite-and-terrestrial system that keeps people connected across locations. Qualcomm executive Durga Malladi emphasized performance and power efficiency at individual sites. Those are stated design objectives, not independently measured results from a completed nationwide deployment.

The main dispute concerns whether this approach is sufficient to compete with Verizon, AT&T and T-Mobile, rather than whether Qualcomm has signed an agreement. Axios and RCR both reported analyst concerns about the expense and coverage obligations of a nationwide build. Their reporting also distinguishes possession of radio technology from possession of enough spectrum and infrastructure to serve customers at competitive scale.

In RCR’s account, analyst Michael Thelander of SRG Research said advanced antenna technology can improve capacity but does not eliminate constraints from limited spectrum. He also questioned whether the proposed radio configuration would be practical at every location. His assessment concerns the operating trade-offs of the design, not a finding that the network cannot be built.

Axios supplied a separate economic assessment from Joe Madden of Mobile Experts. Madden expects a newly built network to have efficiency advantages over incumbents burdened with legacy equipment, but questioned whether smartphone subscriptions alone could justify the investment. That is an analyst’s business-case judgment, not a spending commitment or customer forecast issued by SpaceX.

The contrast is therefore more specific than enthusiasm versus skepticism. The companies describe an integrated network and a technology path to building it; outside analysts question the spectrum, physical coverage and revenues needed to make that network competitive. The available reporting does not establish that either the intended nationwide performance or the anticipated economic advantages have been demonstrated.

There is also a distinction between building independent infrastructure and purchasing access to someone else’s. Axios reported analyst David Barden’s view that a wholesale agreement with an existing carrier would be faster and cheaper than constructing an independent network. RCR’s coverage similarly discusses access to incumbent networks as a way to address gaps during a buildout. Such arrangements are possible business structures, not agreements established by this week’s Qualcomm announcement.

Reports of acquisition discussions do not change the status of the confirmed development. The announced transaction is a technology-supply agreement, with financial terms undisclosed, CNBC reported. Neither that agreement nor the planned site count establishes a completed acquisition, a nationwide retail launch or the commercial terms subscribers would receive.

The scheduled milestone to watch is the beginning of deployment in 2027, reported consistently across the four outlets. RCR said the companies had not provided a more precise schedule or phase-by-phase site totals. That leaves a defined start window, but not a dated nationwide completion commitment; beginning installation and delivering competitive nationwide service remain separate milestones.

Sources: CNBC · Axios · The Verge · RCR Wireless News · Axios

Security & Intelligence

US strikes and tanker incidents keep Hormuz at the center of Iran talks

Limited American strikes on Iran’s Qeshm Island this week brought the military confrontation around the Strait of Hormuz back into public view while mediators continued pursuing an agreement. Separately, reports of vessels struck in the Gulf and Iranian statements that tankers had hit mines showed that the shipping conflict remained active. Neither the description of the strikes as limited nor the continuation of diplomacy amounted to an announced settlement.

The week’s central development was the overlap between military operations and negotiations, rather than a publicly declared return to a larger air campaign. American officials described the November 2 strikes as defensive responses to Iranian threats against aircraft and shipping. Iranian officials characterized American military action as aggression and maintained that Tehran retained a right to defend itself.

Hormuz connects the Persian Gulf with the Gulf of Oman and is a major route for energy exports. Al Jazeera reported that the waterway normally carries about one-fifth of global oil consumption; the BBC likewise described its importance to global oil and gas shipments. Restrictions on passage therefore affect commercial vessels beyond the two countries conducting the confrontation.

The immediate background is a war that began with American and Israeli strikes on Iran in late February. CBS and Al Jazeera described the subsequent targeting of commercial shipping and restrictions on passage. Alongside Iranian pressure on vessels, the United States has maintained a naval blockade intended to prevent Iranian oil tankers from reaching world markets, according to NBC and BBC reporting.

Those are distinct military measures, not interchangeable descriptions of a single closure. Iran has sought to control the routes vessels use through the strait, while American forces have sought to protect shipping and restrict Iranian exports. The BBC described tankers using a southern route through Omani waters to avoid Iran’s preferred passage, and Iranian statements this week again referred to vessels using routes Tehran considered unauthorized.

On Monday, American forces struck Qeshm, an Iranian island in the northern part of the strait. NBC reported, citing American officials, that Iran had tried to target a US aircraft and that the American response struck a missile launcher. CBS independently reported that Revolutionary Guard forces fired at an American fighter jet, prompting it to return fire; Reuters also reported strikes against surface-to-air missile threats.

CBS described another incident later Monday in which Iran fired a missile from Qeshm that threatened shipping. The American officials cited by NBC placed the strike within continuing operations, saying American forces were intercepting missiles and drones and occasionally attacking launchers in self-defense. These accounts establish what American officials said about their actions; they do not constitute an independently published reconstruction of every launch and interception.

President Donald Trump subsequently acknowledged a small attack while continuing to distinguish it from a wider war, NBC reported. He had said the United States would not launch strikes before the midterm elections, NBC reported, and two unnamed sources told Reuters he was still likely to order resumed hostilities. The public distinction was therefore about the scope of operations, not an assertion that all fighting around the strait had stopped.

Iran’s account emphasized a different justification. CBS reported that Deputy Foreign Minister Kazem Gharibabadi asserted Tehran’s right to legitimate self-defense until military aggression ended. NBC reported that parliamentary speaker and negotiator Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf said Iran had delivered its response to Washington and that preventing war was now America’s responsibility. The competing statements assigned responsibility differently rather than resolving the dispute over the confrontation.

Commercial incidents continued alongside those exchanges. Al Jazeera and Reuters reported that the United Kingdom Maritime Trade Operations agency received a report of a tanker struck by two unidentified projectiles off Qatar. Al Jazeera placed the incident about 50 nautical miles north of Madinat ash-Shamal, late Wednesday, and said the agency did not identify the vessel or its flag.

That report should be distinguished from Iranian military statements about mines. On Thursday, Iranian forces said multiple tankers and a tugboat had caught fire in or near the strait; Reuters and Al Jazeera carried those statements. The reported causes included mines, but the Iranian statements were not independent verification of the cause, identity or condition of each vessel. The British report of projectiles did not by itself establish who was responsible.

The distinction also prevents different incidents from being added together as though they formed a single independently confirmed damage assessment. A vessel operator’s report, a maritime warning and a belligerent’s statement answer different questions. Reporting that an attack occurred does not necessarily establish the weapon used, the attacker or whether the ship could continue sailing.

Diplomacy continued during the same period. Reuters and the BBC reported that Qatar said both sides were reviewing proposals presented by mediators; Al Jazeera also reported that talks were continuing. NBC quoted Vice President JD Vance expressing optimism about a negotiated settlement on Monday. These were statements about ongoing discussions, not announcements that Washington and Tehran had accepted common terms.

The disagreement concerned both shipping and the conditions for negotiations. Reuters described disputes over the strait, the American blockade, sanctions and Iran’s nuclear program, while BBC reporting likewise identified blockade relief and access to frozen Iranian assets among Tehran’s demands. Iran’s insistence on conditions before reopening passage differed from Washington’s insistence that nuclear issues belong in the negotiations. No published agreement in the cited reporting reconciled those positions.

For the immediate outlook, the cited reports supplied no jointly announced date for reopening the strait, no scheduled signing ceremony and no agreed timetable for a new negotiating round. There is consequently no confirmed diplomatic deadline to place on the calendar. In the cited reporting, the established developments were continuing mediation, acknowledged limited American strikes and separately attributed shipping incidents—not an announced end to the confrontation.

Construction, Infrastructure & Manufacturing

Data-center construction grows as voters demand tighter controls

Two developments this week exposed the construction industry's dependence on data centers and the growing political resistance to building them. On November 2, new federal spending figures showed server facilities and related power projects supporting an otherwise uneven market. On November 3, voters backed restrictions on data-center development and elected governors who had campaigned for pauses in major construction markets, according to CNBC, Reuters and Construction Dive.

The developments concern different stages of the same building pipeline. September's construction figures measure work already being carried out, while election commitments concern the permissions and conditions governing future projects. Neither the spending release nor the election results establish that the current building boom has ended; analysts distinguished projects already under construction from developments still awaiting approvals.

Total U.S. construction spending fell 1.9% in September from August and 2.7% from September 2025, according to the Census Bureau's November 2 release. Engineering News-Record reported the same declines, alongside a sixth consecutive monthly increase in nonresidential spending. The national totals therefore concealed a substantial difference between residential work and the commercial and public projects outside housing.

Private nonresidential spending increased 1.1% during September, while total nonresidential spending, including public work, rose 0.6%. Associated General Contractors of America and Associated Builders and Contractors separately identified data centers and power as the sources of support. AGC calculated that data-center construction spending rose 8.6% during the month and 90% over the year; Construction Dive also reported those figures.

The comparison with other building categories was pronounced. AGC said private electric-power investment rose 1.3% from August and 14% from a year earlier, while all other private nonresidential categories combined fell 0.2% monthly and 7.8% annually. Those figures describe spending, rather than a count of completed facilities or the computing capacity they will eventually supply.

The contractors' associations also described pressures on delivering that work. AGC economist Ken Simonson said demand for data centers and power was masking investment cutbacks elsewhere while adding to supply-chain and cost strains associated with tariffs and international conflicts. ABC economist Anirban Basu said larger contractors were better positioned to participate in data-center and power projects, according to the association's analysis and Construction Dive's reporting.

The election introduced a different constraint: public consent for new facilities. CNBC reported that 41 local data-center ballot measures appeared across 10 states, with voters favoring restrictions or rejecting development measures in nearly every contest with results available on November 4. Harwood, North Dakota, was the exception identified in its report; Construction Dive independently described widespread support for prohibitions and moratoriums.

Statewide races extended the issue beyond municipal zoning. Reuters and CNBC reported that Texas governor-elect Gina Hinojosa had advocated a statewide moratorium on new approvals. Reuters said she wanted approvals stopped until the legislature passed protections giving residents a role in deciding whether and how data centers enter their communities; that campaign position is not itself an enacted statewide law.

Reuters also reported that outgoing Texas Governor Greg Abbott had already restricted new grid connections and ordered an audit before the election. The dispute therefore did not begin with the change in party control. CNBC's reporting on Hinojosa's proposed approach said projects already under construction would reportedly be exempt, another distinction between the existing buildout and the next wave of applications.

The disagreement centers partly on who pays for supporting infrastructure. Temple University professor Subodha Kumar told Construction Dive that residents were resisting the costs associated with data centers, rather than necessarily rejecting artificial intelligence. He argued that municipalities should require developers to cover land, electricity and water costs and explain those arrangements to residents.

The infrastructure negotiation extends beyond the server buildings themselves. Baker Tilly infrastructure adviser Andy Roehr told Construction Dive that municipalities could seek improvements to bridges used by construction traffic, as well as payment for electricity transmission and distribution. Capstone policy analyst Ishyan Veluppillai said the results would encourage more proactive zoning changes and possible temporary pauses; he also emphasized early, transparent engagement with officials by developers. Those comments describe proposed responses, not requirements already adopted everywhere.

Other advisers emphasized the benefits and the risks of restrictive rules. Roehr told Construction Dive that developers would favor places easier to work with and warned that slower development could affect state and local budgets. Baringa adviser Ben Heininger distinguished relatively limited city-level restrictions from more consequential statewide moratoriums, while arguing for clearer rules and engagement between developers and local officials.

The timetable is also disputed, rather than settled by the vote. CNBC reported Nomura's assessment that much of the capacity expected in 2027 was already under construction, leaving projects planned for 2028 more exposed to permitting delays. Data Center Dynamics separately reported BMI's conclusion that 2027 construction schedules were largely locked in and that the greater uncertainty concerned 2028 and later developments.

BMI did not describe a national shutdown as its central expectation. According to Data Center Dynamics, the research firm regarded a broad national moratorium as unlikely and anticipated more intervention at the state and local levels. These are analyst assessments of potential policy effects, not announced cancellations or measurements of lost construction work.

The same report described two possible effects on investment totals. BMI said fewer projects and postponed starts would reduce spending, but stricter power-procurement requirements or taxes could increase the cost of facilities that proceed. A higher dollar investment total would therefore not, on its own, resolve whether developers were building more capacity or paying more to construct it.

Two scheduled milestones provide the next fixed checks. The Census Bureau lists December 1 for its October construction-spending report, which will cover work before the election rather than measure the effect of incoming administrations. Texas's 90th regular legislative session begins January 12, 2027, according to state legislative calendars; it is a scheduled forum for legislation, not a promised date for adopting Hinojosa's proposed protections or ending a pause.

Science & Health

Congo’s Ebola outbreak tests the limits of existing vaccines and border controls

Two vaccine candidates designed specifically for Bundibugyo Ebola moved toward outbreak testing this week, while a British doctor infected in Congo was evacuated to London and new American air-travel requirements took effect. These developments concern the same public-health problem: controlling a large epidemic when the available Ebola vaccines have not been established as effective against the virus causing it.

More than 10,000 infections and 5,000 deaths have been reported since the outbreak began in May, according to reporting by the BBC and AFP, alongside World Health Organization updates. The Democratic Republic of the Congo bears the overwhelming burden. Rather than treating differing daily tallies as a precise real-time measure, those thresholds describe the scale consistently documented across this week’s reporting.

The distinction between Ebola viruses is central to the response. The outbreak is caused by Bundibugyo virus, not the Zaire Ebola virus targeted by the established Ervebo vaccine. There is no approved vaccine specifically for Bundibugyo disease, and using Ervebo against it remains an efficacy question being investigated, not an extension of its demonstrated protection against another virus.

On November 4, WHO announced that its expert advisers recommended adding two candidates to the ongoing Tumaini vaccine trial in Congo. One is ChAdOx1, developed by the University of Oxford with the Serum Institute of India; the other is Moderna’s mRNA-1469. WHO and independent reporting by CIDRAP and the BBC describe national approvals as the next requirement before the candidates enter the trial.

That qualification matters: a recommendation for clinical evaluation is not regulatory approval for routine vaccination. WHO said the recommendation rested on preclinical and early clinical evidence. It did not announce that either candidate had prevented Bundibugyo infections in the outbreak, nor did it report a completed comparison establishing how much protection either vaccine provides.

Tumaini is led by Congo’s national biomedical research institute with international partners. It is designed to assess multiple candidates within one trial through ring vaccination, enrolling contacts of people with confirmed Bundibugyo disease. As WHO and CIDRAP explain, Ervebo is already being evaluated within that framework to determine whether it offers protection against this virus.

The design focuses research on people linked to known cases rather than treating vaccination as a population-wide intervention already supported by results. A ring identifies an exposure network; it is not itself evidence that a vaccine works. The current announcements describe an ongoing clinical trial, not a peer-reviewed efficacy paper, and do not provide enrolled participant totals or completed effectiveness estimates.

Treatment research is proceeding separately. WHO and CIDRAP report that the PARTNERS trial is evaluating the antibody treatment MBP134, the antiviral remdesivir, and their combination to determine whether they improve survival in confirmed Bundibugyo patients. These are questions under investigation, not demonstrated treatment benefits; the announcements provide neither completed outcome data nor enrolled sample sizes on which to judge an effect. The absence of an approved virus-specific treatment does not mean there is no care: CDC guidance says early supportive care improves survival, consistent with WHO’s emphasis on early care.

The immediate response therefore continues to depend on measures that do not require a successful new vaccine. WHO identifies early care, contact tracing, isolation, safe burials and engagement with communities as essential. Africa CDC likewise emphasizes surveillance, testing, infection prevention and cross-border cooperation, distinguishing those activities from restrictions applied to travellers without a documented exposure.

The evacuation to Britain illustrates another part of that response. A doctor who tested positive while working on the Congo epidemic arrived in the United Kingdom on Thursday, November 5, and was taken to the Royal Free Hospital’s specialist isolation unit in London. The BBC and Guardian independently reported the transfer and the government’s assessment that risk to the wider public remained low.

That assessment does not mean Ebola is harmless. The BBC and AFP describe transmission through direct contact with infected bodily fluids, such as blood or vomit. UK health authorities attributed the low public risk to established infection-prevention procedures and specialist care, rather than suggesting that importing a patient and allowing uncontrolled community exposure are equivalent situations.

The United States took a broader approach to air travel. At 11:59 p.m. Eastern time on November 3, a requirement took effect for everyone boarding an international flight to the United States to attest that they had not been in Congo during the previous 21 days. CDC guidance and Delta’s implementation notice confirm that the requirement is not confined to Congolese nationals or flights originating in Congo.

CDC says the measure is intended to reduce the risk of Ebola entering the United States through air travel, with the waiting period reflecting the maximum incubation period. The agency continues to characterize the overall risk to the American public as low.

African health officials dispute the value of broad restrictions as an outbreak-control tool. In a November 3 statement concerning Saudi restrictions on Kenya, Africa CDC argued that disproportionate measures can disrupt cooperation and livelihoods and discourage transparent reporting. WHO separately advises against international travel and trade restrictions, warning that border closures can redirect movement toward informal, unmonitored crossings.

Those are attributed policy positions, not evidence that every screening measure is ineffective. Africa CDC supports targeted action for cases, suspected cases and exposed contacts; the American order instead uses recent presence anywhere in Congo as its boarding criterion. The disagreement concerns the proportionality and consequences of those approaches, alongside their shared stated objective of limiting transmission.

The next decision points are defined but not all dated. Congolese national approval is required before the two additional vaccine candidates enter Tumaini, and WHO expects vaccine-trial results in early 2027 rather than immediately. The American attestation requirement is scheduled to remain in effect through December 18, subject to amendment or rescission; that is the published policy endpoint, not a forecast for when the outbreak will end.
