Science & Health

Engineered protein "glue" cuts chromosome errors in older mouse eggs

Max Planck Institute researchers reported Thursday in Science that an engineered protein system, TALhesin, can stand in for cohesin, the natural protein that holds paired chromosome strands together and is lost from eggs with age. In older mice, the share of eggs without premature strand separation rose from 46.7% to 74.1% with TALhesin, close to the 74.7% in young mice. In donated human eggs it bound chromosomes and released them on cue. Lead researcher Melina Schuh said it is not yet tested for reducing errors in human eggs.

Sources: Science · Max Planck Institute for Multidisciplinary Sciences · STAT

Level Up

Quiz yourself instead of rereading

When you need to remember material, such as a briefing or a course module, close the notes after one read and write down everything you can recall, then check what you missed. In a 2006 Washington University study, students who repeatedly tested themselves on a passage recalled 61% of it a week later, versus 40% for students who only reread it, though the rereaders read it about 14 times. A 2013 review of 10 study techniques rated practice testing among the two most useful and rereading and highlighting among the least.

Sources: Washington University in St. Louis · Association for Psychological Science

With long documents, put the question last and ask for quotes first

When you hand an AI model a long contract, report or transcript, paste the document at the top of the prompt and put your question and instructions at the end. Anthropic says queries placed at the end improved response quality by up to 30% in its tests, especially with several documents, and Google's Gemini guide likewise says to put the question after all other context. Anthropic also recommends asking the model to pull out the relevant quotes first and then answer based on them; this keeps it anchored to the document.

Sources: Anthropic · Google

Today's field: Science & technology

  • Half-life. A half-life is the time it takes for half of the atoms in a sample of a radioactive substance to decay into something else. Each radioactive isotope has its own fixed half-life, ranging from millionths of a second to billions of years. After one half-life, half the original atoms remain; after two, a quarter; after three, an eighth. Carbon-14, for example, has a half-life of about 5,730 years, which is why measuring how much is left in old bones or wood lets scientists estimate their age. [U.S. Nuclear Regulatory Commission · Encyclopaedia Britannica]
  • Doppler effect. The Doppler effect is the change in a wave's frequency that an observer notices when the source of the wave and the observer are moving toward or away from each other. As an ambulance approaches, its sound waves bunch up and the siren sounds higher; once it passes, the waves stretch out and the pitch drops. Light behaves the same way: light from an object moving away shifts toward red, and from one approaching shifts toward blue. The effect, first described by Austrian physicist Christian Doppler in 1842, is used in radar, navigation and astronomy. [Encyclopaedia Britannica · NASA]
