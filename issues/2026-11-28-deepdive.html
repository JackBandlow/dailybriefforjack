Politics & Elections

Senate Democrats prepare their first contested leader election since 1994

Senate Democrats are scheduled to choose their leader for the new Congress by secret ballot on Tuesday, Dec. 1. Chuck Schumer of New York, who has led the caucus since 2017, faces a challenge from Chris Van Hollen of Maryland. It is the first contested election for the party's Senate leader since 1994.

The race grows out of the Nov. 3 midterms. Democrats flipped nine Republican-held Senate seats, the last of them on Nov. 17, when Mary Peltola defeated Republican Sen. Dan Sullivan in Alaska. That gives the party 56 seats to Republicans' 44 when the new Congress begins in January, its largest Senate majority since 2009. Democrats had not gained nine or more Senate seats in a midterm since 1958.

The new majority turns the minority leader's job into the majority leader's job. The majority leader decides which bills reach the Senate floor and when, so the choice shapes what a Democratic Senate will vote on during the last two years of President Trump's term.

Van Hollen, 67, announced his challenge on the night of Thursday, Nov. 12. "The American people called for change," he said, adding that "to do that we need new leadership." He has served in Congress since 2003, first in the House and since 2017 in the Senate. About a week before entering the race he had said he was exploring a 2028 presidential bid; on Nov. 13 he said he was no longer considering one and would focus on the leadership contest.

Schumer, 75, answered the same night with a statement saying, "I have the votes to continue serving as Democratic Leader." He called "55 seats and counting" an "amazing result," before the Alaska race was called, and has told reporters, "I'm going to win it." He is seeking a sixth two-year term as leader.

The leader is elected by the caucus, not by the full Senate, and the vote takes place behind closed doors. Returning senators and senators-elect vote; senators who are leaving at the end of this Congress do not. That means newly elected Democrats who have not yet been sworn in have a say. Because the ballot is secret, public endorsements are not binding, and no senator has to disclose how they voted.

A candidate needs a simple majority of those voting. Outlets have put that figure at 28 or 29 votes depending on how many members they count as eligible; the Portland Press Herald says 28 if all Senate Democrats vote, while MS NOW has said 29. Either way, the margin between the two counts is a single vote.

The last contested race shows how close these contests can be. In 1994, after Majority Leader George Mitchell of Maine retired, Tom Daschle of South Dakota defeated Christopher Dodd of Connecticut by one vote. Dodd recalled the margin in a 2004 Senate tribute to Daschle: "I am the individual who lost to him by one vote."

Public commitments still leave much of the caucus unaccounted for. At least 16 senators and senators-elect have publicly backed Schumer, according to the Washington Post, which counted Sen. Mark Warner of Virginia among them after he endorsed Schumer on Wednesday. Newsweek, citing a Post tally, reported that 30 of 55 members had said nothing publicly. Van Hollen said on Nov. 22 that "north of 15" senators support him, a figure not reflected in public endorsements; Newsweek counted six public endorsements for him as of that day.

The most notable move this week came Wednesday, Nov. 25, when Maine Sen.-elect Troy Jackson endorsed Van Hollen. Jackson, who defeated Republican Sen. Susan Collins, had said during his campaign that he would not back Schumer. He said he and Van Hollen agree on ending the filibuster, passing Medicare for All and raising the minimum wage. "Mainers voted for change in November, and they ought to get it," Jackson said. Schumer's office did not respond to the Press Herald's request for comment.

Jackson's endorsement put Maine's two senators on opposite sides. Angus King, the state's independent senator who caucuses with Democrats, backs Schumer and has asked, "Why would you fire the manager when you just won the World Series?" Activists delivered a letter with about 1,800 signatures to King's Portland office asking him to switch to Van Hollen, the Press Herald reported.

Van Hollen's other public supporters include Sen. Bernie Sanders of Vermont, an independent who caucuses with Democrats; Sen. Jeff Merkley of Oregon; Michigan Sen.-elect Abdul El-Sayed; and Minnesota Sen.-elect Peggy Flanagan. Schumer's public backers include King and Sens. Jacky Rosen and Catherine Cortez Masto of Nevada, Mazie Hirono of Hawaii and Tammy Baldwin of Wisconsin, who echoed King: "You win the World Series, you don't fire Pat Murphy."

The clearest policy difference is the filibuster. Senate rules require 60 votes to end debate on most legislation, which lets a minority block bills that have majority support. Van Hollen calls the rule "anti-democratic" and says a simple majority should decide: "So, that means 51 votes." He has also criticized Schumer for limiting floor votes on bills popular with Democrats, including one to strengthen unions. The two also differ on the Iran war: Van Hollen wants Congress to cut off funding for it now, while Schumer has said Democrats should use their spending power to end it without setting out a plan, Bloomberg Government reported.

Schumer has not proposed abolishing the filibuster. In 2022 he tried and failed to change the rule narrowly to pass a voting rights package.

Supporters of each candidate also disagree about Schumer's record. Critics point to March 2025, when Schumer voted with Republicans to advance a government spending bill, a decision that angered many Democrats. He later led the caucus through a government shutdown in fall 2025. Supporters argue the midterm results are the measure that counts, which is the point of the World Series comparisons from King and Baldwin.

Schumer also holds the advantages of an incumbent. He has built a support network over nearly a decade as leader and can offer colleagues leadership posts, Fox News noted. PBS NewsHour's Lisa Desjardins said Schumer has "been lining up these votes for some time" and that Van Hollen "announced relatively late in the game."

The confirmed next step is the Dec. 1 secret ballot. The winner will become majority leader when the new Congress is sworn in on Jan. 3, 2027.

AI

Anthropic's first week as a public company: a record IPO, a dip below the offer price and a rebound

Anthropic, the company behind the Claude family of AI models, ended its first full week on the stock market on Friday, Nov. 27, with shares at $91.88. That was a gain of almost 11% on the day and about 10.7% above the $83 price at which it sold stock in its initial public offering, though still below its first-day close. In between, the shares closed below the offer price on two straight days.

The listing had been building for a month. Anthropic filed publicly to go public on Oct. 26, and on Nov. 10 it set a price range of $70 to $78 a share, which TechCrunch calculated would value it at up to about $1.4 trillion on a fully diluted basis. On the evening of Nov. 18 it priced the offering at $83, above that range, and enlarged it from a planned 825 million shares to about 980 million. The sale raised $81.3 billion, according to CNBC, passing SpaceX's June offering, which raised $75 billion at first and $85.7 billion after underwriters exercised an option to sell extra shares.

Demand exceeded supply: CNBC reported that roughly half of the institutional investors who placed orders received no shares. Each of the seven co-founders sold 3.38 million shares, for combined proceeds of about $1.96 billion. Separately, Google, Microsoft, Nvidia and AMD agreed to invest a further $26 billion at the IPO price. At $83, Anthropic's fully diluted market value was about $1.5 trillion.

Trading began on Thursday, Nov. 19, under the ticker ANT on the Nasdaq. The stock opened at $111.50, reached about $115 during the day and closed at $97.17, up 17% from the offer price. Outlets put the first-day value at roughly $1.75 trillion to $1.8 trillion on a fully diluted basis, depending on the share count used.

On Monday, Nov. 23, most AI-related stocks rose, but CNBC noted that Anthropic did not join the rally. On Tuesday, Nov. 24, Anthropic fell 6.5% and closed below the offer price for the first time, as chipmakers and cloud-computing companies also slipped. On Wednesday the shares fell about 3% more, trading near $79, and again closed under $83. Friday's rebound came in a shortened post-Thanksgiving session, during a broad recovery in technology shares, and Forbes counted it as only the third positive day since the debut.

How can an oversubscribed offering trade below its price within a week? In an IPO, the company and its bank underwriters collect orders from large investors and pick a price that clears them; investors who were shut out can then buy on the open market, which often lifts a stock early on. CNBC reported that the shut-out institutions did not appear to be rushing to buy as the price fell, which left trading to be driven by the wider mood in AI stocks.

That mood has been unsettled. CNBC described investors questioning whether AI developers can fund the large data-center build-outs they have planned, and said that doubt was feeding into earnings estimates for chipmakers, cloud providers and infrastructure suppliers. CNBC also cited data from HB Wealth showing that all but five of the 20 largest traditional U.S. IPOs since 1990 fell below their offer price at some point in their first year.

According to its amended prospectus, Anthropic had revenue of $33.2 billion in the nine months to Sept. 30, up roughly 1,200% from a year earlier. It reported a net loss of about $104 billion for the period, but CNBC and City A.M. reported that most of it came from non-cash accounting changes in the value of convertible notes and similar financing instruments rather than from running the business; the operating loss in the third quarter alone was about $567 million. The company also reported run-rate revenue of roughly $81 billion at the end of October, up about $10 billion from a month earlier.

Run-rate revenue is not money already earned. It annualizes recent usage and subscription fees on the assumption that the pace continues for a full year, and the company presents it as a measure outside standard accounting rules, not as a forecast. The filing also lists about $895 billion in purchase and lease commitments, almost entirely for computing infrastructure and data centers, with about $448 billion due by the end of 2030, City A.M. reported.

Control of the company is also unusual. Anthropic remains a Delaware public benefit corporation, a legal form that requires directors to weigh a stated public mission alongside shareholder returns. Its seven co-founders, Dario Amodei, Daniela Amodei, Tom Brown, Jack Clark, Jared Kaplan, Sam McCandlish and Christopher Olah, hold a single Class F share through a "Founder LLC" that carries 50.1% of shareholder voting power on major decisions. A separate body, the Long-Term Benefit Trust, elects four of the seven board seats. The filing warns that decisions made under this structure "may conflict with shareholders' financial interests."

Views on the first week divide. Jim Cramer wrote that the weak trading "might doom" OpenAI's attempts to raise money, and CNBC's Investing Club noted that the company's future commitments far exceed the well over $200 billion in cash it holds. The same CNBC analysis argued the slide may simply reflect normal price discovery, and noted that demand for AI computing still exceeds supply.

The OpenAI link is why the stock matters beyond one company. OpenAI was valued at $1.25 trillion in a recent private funding round, CNBC reported. Its chief financial officer, Sarah Friar, said in August that it "will be a public company in 2027" or sooner, while chief executive Sam Altman ruled out a 2026 listing. A public competitor's share price gives investors a live benchmark for those private valuations, and Anthropic's own shares are a currency it can use for future fundraising and deals.

The next scheduled event is Dec. 15, the first of several dates on which more shares held by pre-IPO investors become eligible for sale under a staggered lock-up schedule set out in the prospectus. Eligibility sets a ceiling, not a forecast, on how many shares are actually sold.

Tech

FCC clears SpaceX to put up to 1 million data-center satellites in orbit

On Wednesday, November 25, the Federal Communications Commission's Space Bureau authorized SpaceX to deploy and operate a constellation of up to 1 million satellites designed to work as data centers in orbit, a system SpaceX calls Starmind. The order, signed by Space Bureau chief Courtney Reinhard, granted the application in part, deferred one key piece and denied petitions to block it from Amazon's satellite unit, satellite operator Viasat and the American Astronomical Society.

SpaceX's Starlink internet network, the largest constellation ever built, has just over 11,000 satellites in space, NBC News reported. Starmind would be roughly 90 times Starlink's current size.

SpaceX filed the application on January 30, 2026, and the FCC accepted it for public comment on February 4. Petitions to deny were filed March 6. Amazon's petition called the plan a "lofty ambition rather than a real plan" and said building it would take "centuries." FCC Chairman Brendan Carr responded in March that Amazon should focus on its own shortfall against a satellite deployment milestone. Blue Origin, the rocket company founded by Amazon's Jeff Bezos, filed its own request in March for a 51,600-satellite computing constellation called Project Sunrise; that application is still pending.

The idea behind orbital data centers is power. In its filing, SpaceX argued that satellites can harness "near-constant solar power with little operating or maintenance costs" and reduce the environmental footprint of data centers on Earth. Google, which is studying a similar concept called Project Suncatcher, has said solar panels in the right orbit can generate up to eight times as much energy as panels on the ground.

The orbits matter for that reason. The FCC approved six nominal operating bands between about 550 and 1,002 kilometers up, three inclined about 30 degrees to the equator and three in sun-synchronous orbits. A sun-synchronous orbit keeps a satellite in the same position relative to the sun; Google's design uses a "dawn-dusk" version that rides the line between day and night, so the panels stay in near-constant sunlight.

What the FCC actually licensed is narrower than the headline. The commission regulates radio spectrum, not computing or business plans. Starmind satellites are meant to talk to each other, and to Starlink, mainly by laser links. The license covers only backup and emergency radio links for tracking and controlling the satellites if those optical links fail, in two Ka-band frequency ranges. SpaceX must use them on a non-interference basis, meaning it cannot claim protection from other operators or cause them harmful interference.

The order waived several standard rules. The FCC let SpaceX skip a "processing round," the process that normally invites competing applicants to share a band. It waived the requirement to post a surety bond and applied a deployment schedule the commission recently adopted. Under it, SpaceX must have one satellite operating by November 25, 2033, 10% of the constellation by November 25, 2035, half by November 25, 2038 and all of it by November 25, 2040, or the license lapses.

Space debris is where the FCC held back. It found that SpaceX's debris plan complies with its rules for satellites operating below 600 kilometers, where atmospheric drag pulls dead spacecraft down relatively quickly. SpaceX had also asked for a waiver of the rule that satellites ending missions below 2,000 kilometers must re-enter the atmosphere within five years. It wanted the option to send higher satellites to disposal orbits or around the sun instead. The bureau deferred that request for lack of information, so SpaceX must file a modification with a full debris plan before deploying any Starmind satellite above 600 kilometers.

SpaceX told the FCC it would test satellites at lower altitudes before raising them, steer re-entries over the ocean and design each satellite to burn up completely. It must also report twice a year, by January 1 and July 1, on close-approach events, failed satellites, re-entries and total deployments.

Amazon and Viasat argued that 1 million satellites could cause aggregate radio interference and raise collision and congestion risks. The FCC said an outage requiring all satellites to use radio at once was unlikely. It said its rules do not cap the number of satellites with similar orbits and that it would not "invent a de facto rule" in a single licensing case.

The American Astronomical Society and groups including Dark Sky International argued for a full environmental review under the National Environmental Policy Act. They also warned about effects on the night sky, and the AAS said waste heat from the satellites could raise the infrared background that astronomers observe. The bureau declined to order that review, citing an October FCC order finding that licensed space-based operations do not trigger the law, and called the infrared concern outside the scope of its radio review. It said SpaceX's commitment to coordinate with the National Science Foundation addressed optical astronomy. The environmental law group Earthjustice criticized the approval for skipping that review and for coming during a holiday week, CNBC reported.

Feasibility is the open engineering question. In a vacuum, heat cannot be carried away by air or water and must be radiated away, which requires large, heavy radiators. Google lists thermal management among the "significant engineering challenges" that remain. Its research estimates that launch prices would need to fall to about $200 per kilogram, which it projects for the mid-2030s, for orbital power costs to be roughly comparable to those on the ground. SpaceX's plan depends on its Starship rocket, still in development, to cut launch costs sharply.

Commenters also argued orbital data centers are inefficient, unnecessary or financially unviable. The bureau said those concerns fall outside its radio mandate and that it would not substitute the government's judgment for that of private business. Carr said the action opens "a bold new frontier for the computing infrastructure" needed for American growth, and Elon Musk thanked him on X.

SpaceX has 30 days from the November 25 release to decline the conditioned grant, or it is deemed accepted. Its first semiannual report is due January 1, 2027. Google, whose first Suncatcher prototype, built with Planet, reached orbit on a SpaceX rideshare launch on October 1, plans to launch two more satellites in 2027 to test laser links between them.

Security & Intelligence

How the Iran war spread to Saudi Arabia and the Gulf's oil infrastructure

In one week, attacks hit or were aimed at Saudi Arabia's largest refinery, its Red Sea refinery at Jazan, its capital and an air defense site in Kuwait, where two Italian civilians were killed. Together they show how a war that began between the United States, Israel and Iran has spread to Gulf states that say they are not fighting it, and why energy infrastructure keeps being hit.

The war began on February 28, 2026, when the United States and Israel launched strikes on Iran, NBC News and CNBC reported. Iran responded with missile and drone attacks on Israel and on Gulf Arab states that host U.S. military bases. A ceasefire in the spring broke down in early July, when Iran's Revolutionary Guards struck commercial ships near Oman, the United States retaliated and the Guards announced the closure of the Strait of Hormuz, according to a Gulf News timeline.

Hormuz matters because before the war about one-fifth of the world's oil and liquefied natural gas passed through it, Al Jazeera reported. Iranian forces say ships using "unauthorised" routes risk hitting naval mines. Washington says the mines have been cleared, and the United States has blocked vessels travelling to and from Iranian ports. In the week ending November 6, the UK Maritime Trade Operations center (UKMTO) said at least 11 ships were attacked in and around the strait and three sank, and that traffic was about 65% below pre-war levels, Al Jazeera reported.

With Hormuz largely shut, Saudi Arabia turned west. The East-West pipeline carries crude across the kingdom to Yanbu on the Red Sea, and Yanbu exported about 4.1 million barrels a day in June, according to Kpler data cited by Al Jazeera. That rerouted roughly 64% of the Saudi oil that would otherwise have gone through Hormuz.

That western route is why Yemen became a second front. The Houthis, an Iran-aligned group, control Sanaa and much of western Yemen and fight the internationally recognised government that Riyadh backs. In July 2026, Yemen's civil war reignited, which NBC News and Al Jazeera describe as a spillover from the U.S.-Iran war. On July 20, the Houthis announced a naval blockade of Saudi Arabia, after threatening a "siege" over an attack on Sanaa's airport, Al Jazeera reported.

Since July, the Houthis have fired missiles and drones at Saudi airports, oil facilities and office buildings. The attacks have temporarily disrupted some Saudi Aramco operations and led to the cancellation of most international flights to Riyadh. Saudi Arabia postponed or cancelled the AFC Asian Cup and the Future Investment Initiative conference, NBC News and Al Jazeera reported. On November 9, the East-West pipeline was attacked again; a Saudi Energy Ministry official said a limited fire was put out and operations were unaffected. On November 19 and again on November 24, strikes hit Riyadh's tallest tower, which houses the headquarters of the Public Investment Fund; the second strike wounded 11 people, NBC News reported.

This week the attacks reached the kingdom's largest energy sites. On November 25, Houthi military spokesperson Yahya Saree said the group attacked Ras Tanura, Saudi Arabia's largest refinery, with a capacity of 550,000 barrels a day, and the Red Sea ports of Jeddah and Jazan. The Saudi-led coalition said air defenses destroyed four drones over Ras Tanura and did not say the refinery was hit. Satellite images from Planet Labs and NASA showed thick black smoke over the site, NBC News reported. Early on November 26, coalition spokesman Turki al-Maliki said air defenses destroyed two ballistic missiles aimed at Riyadh, according to Anadolu Agency and The National.

On November 26, a projectile struck Aramco's Jazan refinery near the Yemeni border, which can process up to 400,000 barrels a day. Saudi Arabia's Energy Ministry said a Nepali worker was killed, several people were injured and some operations were temporarily halted. The ministry did not name the attacker. The coalition said the Houthis carried out the strike and that it destroyed a ballistic missile launch platform in Hodeidah governorate used in the attack.

Saudi-backed government forces said they carried out 767 strikes on Houthi positions on November 26, according to Al Jazeera. The World Health Organization reported 2,361 people killed and 10,982 injured since fighting escalated in August, and the UN said more than 360,000 people have fled their homes.

The Kuwait attack shows how the line between participants and bystanders is disputed. On November 26, two Italian civilian technicians working for Rheinmetall were killed at an air defense surveillance site in Kuwait, where they maintained antennas for the Kuwaiti armed forces' Skyguard system, the Italian Foreign Ministry said. Italian officials said drones struck the site; Kuwait's army said the men were killed by falling debris from Iranian attacks, according to Reuters reporting carried by NBC News, ANSA and CNBC. Foreign Minister Antonio Tajani said a Kuwaiti army captain and a Pakistani national were injured, that Italy would lodge a complaint with Iran's embassy, and that Arab countries not involved in the war should not be attacked. Iran's armed forces spokesperson Abolfazl Shekarchi said Iranian forces carried out no offensive operations in the region. Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi denied any Iranian role, called the incident "suspicious" and offered a joint investigation, NBC News and CNBC reported. Earlier in November, Iran said it targeted U.S. drone and missile sites in Kuwait after U.S. strikes on Qeshm Island in the Strait of Hormuz.

The two sides dispute the causes of many of these incidents. Iran links its attacks to U.S. and Israeli military activity. The Saudi-led coalition and its allies accuse Iran of supporting the Houthis, while the Houthis have said some of their strikes on Saudi Arabia respond to coalition airstrikes in Yemen.

Qatar has been passing proposals between Washington and Tehran, and on November 3 it said efforts to bridge the gap were ongoing and both sides remained committed to mediation, Anadolu Agency and The Jerusalem Post reported. On a Thanksgiving call with U.S. troops, President Donald Trump said the United States was still giving Iran a chance to reach a deal, and he told PBS the United States had "victory at hand," CNBC reported. Israeli military spokesman Effie Defrin said Israel was ready to strike Iran at any moment, even without U.S. participation.

Construction, Infrastructure & Manufacturing

TSMC's U.S. factory buildout moves to a new Arizona phase and a possible Texas site

President Trump is scheduled to travel to Phoenix on Dec. 4 to tour Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Co.'s Arizona site and speak at the groundbreaking for the latest phase of its expansion there, a White House official and a TSMC spokesperson told CBS News. The same week, filings in Texas showed the company weighing a separate $22.6 billion chip plant in Brazos County.

TSMC makes the most advanced processors for companies including Nvidia, Apple, AMD, Broadcom and Qualcomm, and most of that production has been in Taiwan. That concentration has pushed the United States and other countries to seek more domestic chipmaking to reduce supply-chain risk. TSMC began building in Arizona under President Joe Biden, initially committing more than $65 billion to three plants in Phoenix.

In November 2024, the Commerce Department finalized a CHIPS and Science Act award of up to $6.6 billion in direct funding for TSMC Arizona, plus up to $5 billion in proposed loans. Axios reported at the time that finalizing the award protected it even if the incoming Trump administration and congressional Republicans moved to repeal the CHIPS Act.

The commitments then grew in two steps. In March 2025, TSMC added $100 billion, bringing its planned U.S. total to $165 billion. On July 16, 2026, the White House and the Commerce Department announced another $100 billion, raising the total to $265 billion across 12 facilities, which TSMC Chairman C.C. Wei called part of the largest foreign direct investment in U.S. history. According to the City of Phoenix, the latest tranche covers four additional plants making chips at 2 nanometers or more advanced, and about 30% of TSMC's capacity at that level will be in Arizona once the announced plants are complete.

Neither the White House nor TSMC gave a cost or production date for the new phase when announcing the visit. A White House official told CBS News the expansion would create more than 10,000 direct jobs and about 11,000 indirect jobs in the surrounding area. The City of Phoenix said in July that TSMC Arizona employs more than 3,500 people.

Progress on the earlier plants shows the timeline involved. The first Phoenix fab has been in volume production of 4-nanometer chips since late 2024, with yields comparable to its Taiwan plants, according to the city. On its Oct. 15 earnings call, TSMC said construction of the second fab, which will make 3-nanometer chips, is complete and equipment is being moved in and installed, with volume production now set for the third quarter of 2027, slightly ahead of its previous schedule.

The gap between a finished building and a working fab reflects how these plants are built. The shell is a large cleanroom structure, but most of the money goes into the manufacturing tools installed afterward. TSMC's Texas filing illustrates the split: about $8.6 billion for buildings and real property spent in 2027 through 2029, and about $14 billion for machinery and equipment, beginning in 2029 and peaking in 2030, according to Tom's Hardware's review of the documents.

The Texas proposal is the week's other development. TSMC Arizona Corp. applied to the Texas Comptroller under the state's Jobs, Energy, Technology and Innovation program for a school property-tax limit on a fab on about 3,200 acres in southern Brazos County. The plant would make 2-nanometer or more advanced chips for artificial intelligence, high-performance computing and mobile uses, with roughly 2,099 direct jobs projected.

The estimated tax benefit is about $354 million, and TSMC called it a "determinative and compelling factor" in choosing Texas. The filing projects construction starting in 2027 and commercial operation in 2031. The Comptroller noted it has not audited TSMC's projections, and the application does not commit the company to buy land or build.

TSMC also told Texas officials it is still considering Arizona for the project, citing proximity to its existing fabs, workforce and supplier ties, logistics and government relationships. In Arizona, the company bought about 902 additional acres next to its Phoenix campus in January 2026, according to Tom's Hardware. That month, Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick said TSMC's board would decide on expansion there, CNBC reported.

On its October call, Wei said TSMC had sent teams to evaluate Texas and was in talks with a customer building a fab called Terafab about using that capacity. He described nearly 20 fabs in progress worldwide: 13 leading-edge and advanced-packaging fabs in Taiwan and another five to six overseas.

Trade policy is closely tied to the buildout. Under a U.S.-Taiwan agreement announced Jan. 15, 2026, Taiwanese chip and technology companies committed to invest at least $250 billion in the United States, and Taiwan's government pledged $250 billion in credit guarantees. The U.S. "reciprocal" tariff on Taiwanese goods was capped at 15%, down from 20%.

For chips, the deal links tariff relief to U.S. construction under Section 232, the national-security tariff law. Companies building U.S. fabs may import up to 2.5 times their planned capacity without those tariffs during construction, and 1.5 times their U.S. capacity once complete. Lutnick said chipmakers that do not build in the United States would likely face a 100% tariff.

Credit for the investment is disputed. Trump has presented TSMC's U.S. spending as a result of his tariff policy, while construction began under Biden with support from the bipartisan CHIPS Act he signed.

The cost of building abroad is also a point of debate. TSMC said on its October call that overseas plants remain more expensive to run and that they will reduce its gross margin by close to 2 percentage points in 2026, widening to 3 to 4 points in the next few years as more U.S. fabs ramp up. The company said it expects to offset part of that through scale in Arizona and cost improvements.

In Texas, the College Station Independent School District board will hold a public meeting on Dec. 10 as part of the tax-limit process, and both the district and the governor must approve the agreement before it can be signed.

Science & Health

How the US lost its measles elimination status, and why cases are still rising

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) reported 6,039 confirmed measles cases in the United States for 2026 as of November 24, the highest annual total in more than three decades. The agency lists nine measles deaths so far this year and notes that the death count is subject to change. The update came one week after the Pan American Health Organization (PAHO) ruled that measles is again endemic in the US.

The US declared measles eliminated in 2000. Elimination does not mean zero cases; it means the virus no longer spreads continuously inside the country, so outbreaks start with travelers who catch it abroad and then burn out. Under that model the US averaged about 180 cases a year, ABC News reported, and recorded fewer than 300 in 2024.

That changed in 2025, when home-grown outbreaks spread in several states and the year ended with 2,289 confirmed cases, according to the CDC. The 2026 total is already more than double that figure. Of this year's cases, 6,021 were reported in 47 US jurisdictions and 18 were among international visitors, and 97% were linked to outbreaks rather than isolated imported infections.

PAHO's Regional Verification Commission met from November 10 to 13 and concluded that endemic transmission had been re-established in Bolivia, Mexico and the United States. PAHO announced the decision on November 17 after Director Jarbas Barbosa accepted it. The test is narrow and technical: a country loses its status when a single measles genotype and lineage circulates without interruption for 12 months or more. Al Jazeera reported that the review relied on epidemiological and laboratory evidence submitted by the CDC.

Canada lost its status in November 2025. PAHO said Canada has since broken the transmission chain behind that loss and is working to regain elimination. Across the Americas, PAHO counted 58,213 confirmed cases and 82 deaths this year as of November 13, compared with 12,806 cases in the same period of 2025.

The national figure is driven by a few large outbreaks in close-knit communities with low vaccination rates. In mid-November, the University of Minnesota's Center for Infectious Disease Research and Policy (CIDRAP) reported 1,631 cases in Pennsylvania, where an outbreak has run since April. Wisconsin had 832 cases, most among unvaccinated people in Amish and Old Order Mennonite communities, according to state officials quoted by CIDRAP. Minnesota, Indiana and New York each had more than 200 cases.

CIDRAP reported that the CDC added 426 cases in the week the national total reached 5,838, the largest one-week jump in decades and the 15th straight week of triple-digit growth. The November 24 update added 201 more.

About 96% of this year's patients were unvaccinated or had unknown vaccination status, and about 11% were hospitalized, the same share as in 2025, according to CIDRAP's reading of CDC data. Nearly six in 10 patients are children or teenagers.

Measles spreads more easily than almost any other human virus, which is why small gaps in immunity matter. According to the CDC, up to nine out of 10 susceptible people in close contact with a patient will become infected. The virus can stay infectious in the air of a room for up to two hours after the sick person leaves, and patients are contagious from four days before to four days after the rash appears.

Two doses of the measles, mumps and rubella (MMR) vaccine, given at 12 to 15 months and again at four to six years, are about 97% effective at preventing infection. Health officials treat 95% coverage as the level needed to stop sustained spread. A national average near that line can hide towns, schools and congregations far below it.

US coverage has been slipping. CDC data released in August showed 92.4% of kindergartners entering school in 2025-26 had the required MMR doses, down from 95.2% in the 2019-20 school year. Exemptions from at least one school vaccine reached 4.2%, up from 3.6% the year before, and 24 states had exemption rates above 5%, according to CIDRAP. About 280,000 kindergartners started school without proof of completing the MMR series.

Even the death count is unsettled. In mid-November the CDC had confirmed six measles deaths, while state health departments in Pennsylvania, Wisconsin and New York had reported 10, according to ABC News and Al Jazeera. CIDRAP put the state-reported total at 11, eight of them in Pennsylvania, and reported that the CDC is working on a standard definition of a measles death. The CDC's own count has since risen to nine.

Federal officials have played down the significance of the label while backing vaccination. CDC Director Erica Schwartz said, "The measles elimination designation is not what protects Americans. Vaccination does," and said the agency has given state and local health departments about $100 million since August for vaccination, surveillance and outbreak response. Health and Human Services Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. wrote on X: "We will stop measles outbreaks, protect American families, and work to regain U.S. measles elimination status."

Critics have questioned the administration's approach. Al Jazeera reported that Kennedy has suggested MMR protection wanes by "about 4.5 percent a year," and his changes to the childhood vaccine schedule drew criticism from medical groups and lawsuits, according to ABC News.

States are also changing how they protect infants too young for the routine first dose. In June, Pennsylvania's health department recommended an extra early "dose zero" for babies from six months old in affected counties, The Philadelphia Inquirer reported. The early dose gives short-term protection and does not replace the routine doses.

PAHO's commission set priority actions for the three countries, including active case finding, risk assessment and timely resources for outbreak response. To regain elimination, the US must show that the lineages now circulating have been interrupted for at least 12 months. The CDC updates its national case count weekly.
